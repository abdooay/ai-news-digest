## What changed

OpenAI says an internal, unreleased version of its next major model — code-named **Astra** — produced new results on ten long-standing open problems in mathematics and theoretical computer science. The problems span areas including high-dimensional geometry, coding theory, group theory, quantum complexity, lattice cryptography, and combinatorics. OpenAI says each problem had seen no progress on its main question for at least a decade, and that several are of broad interest across mathematics, not just to specialists.

Examples from the list of ten:
- A construction proving that "non-sofic groups" exist, settling a central open question in group theory.
- A disproof of Connes's rigidity conjecture, a long-standing claim in the study of operator algebras.
- A superexponential lower bound for a type of Ramsey number problem (about how large a group must be before a certain pattern is guaranteed to appear), resolving a specific problem posed by mathematician Paul Erdős.
- New, exponentially better bounds for binary and spherical "codes" (a coding-theory concept related to error correction).

According to OpenAI, the model first generated the mathematical arguments. Humans then worked with the same model to write those arguments up as manuscripts. After that, the model formalized each proof as a **Lean certificate** — Lean is software that mechanically checks whether a mathematical proof is logically valid, so a "Lean certificate" is a machine-checkable version of the proof, not just a human-readable one. OpenAI published these certificates on GitHub (github.com/openai/ten-proofs) along with a write-up of the model's reasoning process for each result. OpenAI estimates the total token cost of finding all ten solutions at roughly $2,000 at its Sol API pricing.

OpenAI states plainly that the mathematical arguments were generated by its system, not by the humans involved, and that it takes responsibility for the correctness of the manuscripts and formalizations it helped prepare.

## Why it matters

Lean-certified proofs matter because they remove a big source of doubt about AI-generated math: instead of asking mathematicians to manually check every step of a claimed proof, the proof can be run through Lean, which either confirms it's logically valid or rejects it. That makes it possible to trust a machine-generated proof without redoing all the work by hand — though it still takes real effort to write the proof in a form Lean can check, and passing a Lean check doesn't by itself tell you whether the result is important or interesting.

This is also a signal about where OpenAI's next model, Astra, might be strong: research-level mathematical reasoning, not just exam-style problem solving. Astra itself has not been released; this announcement is about capability results from an internal, unreleased version of it.

## How to use it

- The Lean certificates and proofs are public on GitHub at [github.com/openai/ten-proofs](https://github.com/openai/ten-proofs), so anyone with Lean installed can independently verify them.
- OpenAI also published a narration of the model's reasoning for each result alongside the certificates, useful if you want to see how the arguments were found, not just that they check out.
- Astra itself is not available through the API; this is a research preview of results, not a product launch.
