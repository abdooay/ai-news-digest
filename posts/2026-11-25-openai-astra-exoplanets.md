## What changed

OpenAI published a story on November 24, 2026 about two amateur astronomers who used AI coding tools to search public telescope data for signs of exoplanets (planets outside our solar system).

- **Kevin Bond**, an OpenAI engineer, used Codex (OpenAI's coding agent) to search data from about 100,000 stars observed by NASA's Kepler telescope (which ran 2009–2018). One AI agent scanned for promising brightness dips; a second ran closer checks on what the first flagged. The program flagged over 200 signals, including correctly re-finding 10 already-confirmed planets — a check that the method works.
- **Ethan Byrd**, founder of an email infrastructure startup, used Astra (another AI tool) to run two searches: a broad search for candidates, and a narrower one focused on stars' "habitable zones" — the distance range from a star where a planet could have liquid water on its surface. He searched data from about 19 million stars, mostly from NASA's TESS mission (2018–present). His first pass found 49 candidates, and the search is ongoing.
- The two worked independently and did not use the same tool.

**How it works**: when a planet passes in front of its star as seen from Earth (a "transit"), it blocks a small amount of light, creating a dip on a "light curve" (a graph of a star's brightness over time). A dip that repeats at a regular interval suggests an orbiting planet and tells astronomers when to look again for it.

**Specific candidates from Bond's search**:
- **KIC 5689121**: a small, cool star; a dip repeats every 13.8 days, consistent with a planet about 2.4 times Earth's diameter.
- **KIC 6691030**: a faint star; a dip repeats every 4.8 days, consistent with a planet smaller than Neptune.

An independent astronomer at the SETI Institute reran the search and found the same two signals in the Kepler data. Byrd sent one of his strongest candidates to researchers on NASA's TESS mission for further analysis.

**Important caveat**: these are candidates, not confirmed planets. Confirming a planet requires ruling out other explanations (such as two stars passing in front of each other), using statistics to show a planet is the most likely cause, and ideally cross-checking with another method such as radial velocity (measuring tiny shifts in a star's light caused by a planet's gravity tugging it back and forth). That process can take months or years, and the results must be published so other astronomers can check the evidence.

## Why it matters

This shows AI coding tools letting people without a professional astronomy background design and run their own scientific searches — not just use existing software, but have an AI help write, combine, and improve a search program from published methods. The source data (Kepler, TESS) is public, so the tools are mainly automating the labor of writing and tuning search code, not accessing anything new. If even one of these candidates is confirmed, it would be a concrete example of AI-assisted amateur work contributing a new discovery, not just reproducing known results.

## How to use it

You don't need Bond's or Byrd's exact setup to try this yourself. NASA runs several citizen-science programs that don't require owning a telescope:

- [NASA Exoplanet Watch](https://science.nasa.gov/citizen-science/exoplanet-watch/how-to-contribute/) — request data and learn to plot a light curve.
- [NASA exoplanet citizen science programs](https://science.nasa.gov/exoplanets/citizen-science/) — other ways to get involved.
- [AAVSO Exoplanet Observing Program](https://aavso.org/exoplanet-photometry/) — learn to measure a starlight dip and share your observations.
- [Unistellar Network Investigating TESS Exoplanets](https://science.unistellar.com/exoplanets/unite/) — coordinates volunteers worldwide to observe the same star together.
