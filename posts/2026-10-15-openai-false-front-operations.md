## What changed

On October 8, 2026, OpenAI published a report on two covert influence operations it banned for misusing its models. Both combined AI with traditional propaganda tactics to run "false front" entities — fake organizations or personas built to look independent and credible.

**"Dark Clark" (Russia-linked).** Operators ran a fake Latin American think tank called the "Social Research Center," fronted by a fake persona named "Mia Clark." Most of its activity aimed to undermine support for Ukraine and influence politics in Argentina and Bolivia. The operators used ChatGPT mainly to draft internal reports, which often exaggerated how effective the operation actually was. OpenAI rated this operation Category 5 on the Brookings Breakout Scale (a scale for how far propaganda content spreads into real public discussion) — the first Category 5 operation OpenAI has disrupted. It stood out because it recruited unwitting local staff to help run it, and its fake content drew fact-checks and official government denials.

**"Bogus Bylines" (Iran-linked).** Operators used seven fake journalist personas to pitch long-form articles about the US-Iran conflict to small and medium online outlets, and also mass-generated social media comments. The articles reached Category 4 on the same scale; the social media comments only reached Category 2, with little real engagement. The operation's own internal effectiveness numbers were inflated by a calculation error.

OpenAI says both operations closely resembled pre-AI-era propaganda campaigns — the AI mainly made existing work (translation, drafting, editing) faster, rather than creating new kinds of attacks. Content placed in real media outlets reached far more people than content relying on fake social media accounts. Both operations stopped once OpenAI exposed them, which OpenAI says is part of why it publishes these reports.

## Why it matters

This is a reminder that state-linked propaganda operations are actively trying to use mainstream AI chatbots as production tools — mainly for speed and language work, not for some fundamentally new capability. The detail worth noting for anyone evaluating AI-generated content risk: operations that got real journalists or outlets to publish their content did far more damage than ones that just posted fake social media comments. Fake comments, even AI-generated in bulk, got little traction.

## How to use it

This is a report, not a product change — there's nothing to configure. If you run a newsroom, research organization, or online publication, the practical lesson is to be more cautious about unsolicited pitches from freelance "journalists" or think tanks you can't independently verify, especially ones pushing content about live geopolitical conflicts.
