## What changed

On September 10, 2026, OpenAI published a case study about César de la Fuente, a bioengineer whose lab searches for new antimicrobial molecules (compounds that can fight drug-resistant bacteria, fungi, parasites, and viruses) using AI. Drug-resistant infections were associated with about five million deaths in 2021, a toll that researchers project could roughly double by 2050, and no new class of antibiotics has been discovered in 50 years.

De la Fuente's lab treats biology as an information system — DNA and proteins as a kind of alphabet — and trains its own deep-learning models to recognize patterns in genetic and protein sequences that might indicate a useful molecule. This can shrink the initial search for candidate molecules from years to hours. Separately, lab members use ChatGPT and Codex (OpenAI's coding-focused AI tool) to: brainstorm and refine hypotheses; write and debug code; download, organize, and process large genome datasets; and help team members from different fields (biology, chemistry, computer science) understand each other's work and terminology.

De la Fuente is clear that AI narrows the search but doesn't replace lab work: candidates still have to be tested to confirm they kill the target microbe, checked for safety in human cells, and go through the same regulatory and clinical trial process as any other drug before reaching patients.

## Why it matters

This case study illustrates a common, unglamorous way AI tools get used in scientific research: not as a replacement for the core discovery method (the lab's own specialized deep-learning models), but as a general-purpose assistant that removes friction around it — writing the code to process data, translating between disciplines on a team where not everyone shares the same technical background, and acting as a sounding board while forming a hypothesis. For a field as slow-moving and high-stakes as antibiotic discovery, even modest reductions in the time spent on non-core tasks (writing boilerplate code, searching literature, clarifying jargon across disciplines) can matter given the urgency of rising drug resistance.

## How to use it

This is a research case study rather than a product announcement, so there's no new feature to configure. The practical takeaway for other research teams: general-purpose AI assistants like ChatGPT and Codex can be useful glue between specialized scientific tools and a cross-disciplinary team, even when the core scientific method (in this case, the lab's own trained models) is unrelated to those assistants.
