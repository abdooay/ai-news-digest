## What changed

On September 21, 2026, OpenAI published a blog post called "Building standards for the next phase of AI." In it, the company asks the United States to lead an international effort to build common technical standards for frontier AI — the most capable AI models being built today, especially ones that can do AI research themselves.

The proposed standards would cover:

- **Capability measurement** — common ways to measure and compare what different companies' AI models can actually do.
- **Safeguard evaluation** — standard methods for checking how well safety protections work.
- **Incident classification and reporting** — shared definitions for what counts as a safety "incident," and consistent rules for when and how one must be reported.
- **Human oversight triggers** — clearer rules for deciding when AI systems doing research on AI need a human to step in and check the work.
- **Rules for AI that improves AI** — governance for systems capable of "recursive self-improvement" (RSI), meaning AI that helps design or improve the next generation of AI, before that process runs without human involvement.

OpenAI proposes building on an existing body rather than starting from scratch: the International Network for Advanced AI Measurement, Evaluation, and Science, set up in 2024 through the US Center for AI Standards and Innovation. That network already includes government bodies from the US, UK, EU, Japan, Canada, and other countries.

Importantly, OpenAI's proposal explicitly does not ask for mandatory rules. There would be no licenses required to build AI, no mandatory government review before releasing a model, and no approval process to clear. Countries would decide on their own whether to write these standards into law.

## Why it matters

Right now, different AI labs and different countries each judge safety in their own way, using their own tests and their own definitions of what counts as a problem. That makes it hard to compare how risky one company's AI model is against another's, and hard to coordinate if something goes wrong across borders. A shared measurement and reporting system would let regulators, researchers, and the public compare frontier models on the same terms, and let a warning discovered by one country's evaluators be understood the same way elsewhere.

This proposal is especially aimed at a specific future risk: AI systems that are good enough to do AI research on their own, potentially speeding up how fast more capable AI gets built with less human oversight at each step. OpenAI wants agreed rules for when that process needs a person to check in, before it becomes common.

That said, this is a proposal, not a policy. It has no enforcement mechanism yet, and reports on it note that specific penalties for not following the standards remain unspecified. Nothing changes for developers or users today.

## How to use it

There's nothing to install or turn on — this is a policy proposal aimed at governments and standards bodies, not a product or API change. If you follow AI policy or work at a company that might be affected by future AI regulation, the practical next step is to watch the International Network for Advanced AI Measurement, Evaluation, and Science and the US Center for AI Standards and Innovation for whether they take up OpenAI's proposed framework.
